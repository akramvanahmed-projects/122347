The Netherlands · Two wars, one household budget

Are there human casualties in the Netherlands from the wars in Ukraine and Iran?

No bombs fell here. But a war two thousand kilometres away reached every kitchen, meter cupboard and holiday plan in the country. Counted the way an army counts its losses, the Russia–Ukraine war left the Netherlands with dead from the cold, seriously wounded, a light wound on almost everyone, and waking lifetimes spent watching it happen.

This is how we counted, chapter by chapter, with every source and every assumption in view.

The bill

02The phantom

The inflation everyone heard about was not the inflation most households paid.

Until June 2023, Statistics Netherlands priced energy from new contracts only. In September 2022 the official figure said prices were 14.5% higher than a year before. Recomputed with the contracts people actually had, it was 7.8%. The headline ran months ahead of the bills.

Then the order flipped. As fixed contracts renewed at crisis prices, households paid 11.6% more in January 2023 while the official figure had already fallen to 7.6%. The pain arrived after the news had moved on.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellThe war drove the prices. The gap between the two lines is a measurement problem, and it changes when people felt the shock.

03The kitchen

Energy came back down. Groceries never did.

The average household paid more for food over 22 months than it would have without the shock. By December 2023 food was still above its no-war path, and that higher level is now simply what food costs. Of everything the war did to Dutch prices, the grocery bill is the part that stayed.

Food banks served almost 200,000 people in 2022. By 2025 the number had fallen to about 155,000, still high, and rising again.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellEnergy, fertiliser and grain prices push food up, but so did wages, margins and bird flu in 2022–23. The model counts all excess food inflation, so this is an upper bound.

04Who paid

Support pulled the poorest fifth above the waterline. The middle stayed under.

Every household got €380 off its energy bill in late 2022, and households near the social minimum could claim €1,300 in both 2022 and 2023. For the poorest fifth that was more than the war cost them on average. The middle three fifths, too well off for targeted help, lost between 1.5% and 1.7% of a year's income.

The poorest fifth's gain depends on who claimed. Below take-up it turns into a loss. Municipalities report reaching 86 to 90% of eligible households. The households that missed the payment still lost about €830 each.

Part II

The wounded

People whose lives got harder: lightly, in their thousands of thousands, and seriously, in their hundreds of thousands.

05The cold homes

The country turned the heating down by two degrees and used the least gas since 1972.

Households burned about a quarter less gas in 2022 than in 2021. Weather explains part of it; corrected for the mild winter, use was still 15% lower. Seven in ten households with a gas boiler set the thermostat to 19 °C instead of 21. Smart-thermostat data put the average Dutch living room at 18 °C that winter.

Read that as a casualty count and it is the widest wound of the war: about people spent the winter in a home two degrees colder than the one they were used to. A light wound for most. For an elderly person living alone, or a child with asthma, not light at all. Whether it killed anyone is the question of Part III.

06The nerves
−59

Dutch consumers were never as gloomy as in the autumn of the energy crisis.

Consumer confidence fell to −59 in September 2022, the lowest reading since CBS began measuring in 1986. The twenty-year average is around −10. In the same months, one in three households told Nibud it could barely make ends meet, and calls to its helpline ran at 1.5 to 2 times the usual rate.

In casualty terms these are light wounds, but there were many of them: about adults worried "a lot" about the war in March 2022, and roughly more people than in 2021 worried about money. The worry outlasted the prices. The share of people worried about their finances rose from 23% in 2021 to almost 30%, and to about 40% among the lowest earners. When the Iran strikes began, confidence dropped from −24 in February 2026 to −46 by May.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellThe timing lines up twice, at the energy peak in 2022 and after the strikes in 2026. CBS does not attribute the drops to a cause.

07The holiday

For the lowest incomes, the holiday is the first thing to go.

In 2023, 19.2% of people aged 15 and over took no holiday, and 27.6% of them said they could not afford one. Income splits the country: 38% of the lowest-income quarter stayed home, against 8% of the highest.

Compared with 2019, about more people took no holiday in 2022. That is an upper bound for the war's share, because some of it is the slow return from covid. We count them as lightly wounded.

Did the energy crisis cancel holidays? The national numbers cannot say. Covid emptied the airports in 2020 and the recovery masks any war effect. Iran is easier to see. In April 2026 KLM scrapped 160 flights over the May holiday, and Air France–KLM expects to spend about €2 billion more on kerosene this year, which goes onto ticket prices.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellFor 2022 the covid recovery swamps any war effect. For 2026 the cancelled flights are directly fuel-driven.

CBS, 1 op de 5 Nederlanders ging in 2023 niet op vakantie (2024) · NL Times, 16 Apr 2026

08The serious wounded

Nearly a million people took a serious wound.

Two groups carried the heaviest part of the bill. The first went hungry enough to ask for help: food banks served 147,500 people in 2021 and 197,000 in 2022, almost 50,000 more in the first year of the war.

The second is quieter. In the poorest fifth, the households that did not receive the €1,300 energietoeslag, because they earned slightly too much or never claimed it, still lost about €830 each over the shock. That is roughly 5% of a year's income for people with none to spare. We count about people in that position.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellFood-bank demand also rose with general inflation and the end of covid support. The poorest-fifth count rests on the 55% take-up assumption from chapter 04.

Part III

The dead

Three ways to count lives: deaths from the cold, life expectancy lost to lower income, and healthy years worn away by worry.

09The cold, again

The cold probably killed people. We can estimate how many, not who.

No death certificate in the Netherlands says "energy prices". So we borrow a number from the economics literature. Studying US winters, Chirakijja, Jayachandran and Ong found that a 1% rise in the price of heating raises deaths by about 0.032%. People heat less, and the cold reaches hearts and lungs.

In the Dutch heating season of October 2022 to March 2023, home energy cost on average more than it would have without the war. About 82,000 deaths were expected in those six months. Multiply the three and you get roughly extra deaths, with a plausible range of .

Is that credible? CBS counted about 4,800 more deaths than expected between late November 2022 and January 2023, mostly from flu and covid. Our six-month estimate is about a quarter the size of that two-month excess. For Europe as a whole, The Economist estimated 68,000 deaths linked to high energy prices that winter. Scaled to the Dutch population, that points to a similar order of magnitude.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellThe mechanism is well documented, the size is borrowed from US data. The price ceiling and the €380 discount kept the number lower than the peak prices alone would imply.

Chirakijja, Jayachandran & Ong, The Mortality Effects of Winter Heating Prices, Economic Journal (2023) · CBS, Eind januari geen oversterfte meer (2023) · The Economist (10 May 2023) via American Experiment

10The count

Spread across 18 million people, the war took a few hours of life from each of them.

11The worn-down years

Worry is not fatal, but it costs healthy years. Added up, the wounds equal hundreds of lives.

Health economists price illness in healthy life-years. A year lived with mild anxiety counts as 3% of a healthy year lost; with moderate anxiety, 13%. These are the disability weights of the Global Burden of Disease study. Financial worry is not a diagnosis, so we give it a third of the mild-anxiety weight, and use the full weight only as the upper bound. Serious hardship, like needing food aid, gets twice the mild weight.

Multiply each group of wounded by its weight and by how long the wound lasted. The total is about healthy years, the equivalent of full lifetimes, with a range of . This is the softest number in the story, and the bars show why: most of it comes from millions of people each carrying a small weight.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellThe counts of people are measured or close to it. The weights are our choice, anchored to GBD values, and the result moves one-for-one with them.

Part IV

The hours

What the war took that no statistic records: time.

12The attention

Counted in time, the war took another thousand lifetimes.

In the first weeks of the invasion, 87% of Dutch adults followed the war and 90% were worried about it; 44% feared a nuclear attack. On 7 March 2022 the eight o'clock news drew 2.3 million viewers. Then came a year of talk at kitchen tables, in parliament and at work about gas bills, sanctions and refugees.

Nobody measured how many minutes that took, so the dial below is an assumption you can change. The default: followers spent 15 extra minutes a day on the war in the first three months, and 5 minutes a day for the rest of the year. Multiply that by about 12 million adults and divide by a waking lifetime (82 years of 16 waking hours) and you get the number above.

Time spent understanding a war is not lost the way a life cut short is. Some of it was civic duty, and some of it turned into donations and spare rooms for refugees. But every hour of it was taken from something else.

Part V

The next bill

The Iran war, six months in.

13No umbrella

The Iran shock is small so far. It is landing on households with no support left.

Six months in, motor fuel sits about 15% above its no-war path while household energy has barely moved. Most people are on contracts that reprice over the coming year. The cost so far is per household. The CPB expects no purchasing-power growth this year instead of +1.4%, inflation of 3.8 to 5.3%, and €200–350 a year extra for households just above the poverty line.

The difference from 2022 is the cushion. Energy poverty fell during the Ukraine shock because support kept people out of it. When the payments stopped it went back to 6%, higher than at any point during that war. That is the starting line for Iran.

Can we blame the war?YesPartlyCan't tellFuel prices responded within weeks of the strikes. The bigger effects on gas contracts and food are still to come.

Epilogue

“Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.”

Martin Luther King Jr., Letter from Birmingham Jail, 1963

The war was fought far away. Its bill was paid here, and not evenly.

Every chapter of this story ends in the same place. The poorest fifth lost three times as large a share of its income before support arrived. It was five times as likely to skip a holiday, and twice as likely to lie awake over money. The households that fell through the gap in the support scheme were all in the bottom fifth. The cold, the worry and the lost years landed hardest where the margins were thinnest.

The war's real casualties are in Ukraine and in Iran, and they are of a different order. What this story counts is the echo: a price shock that crossed a continent and found, in one of the richest countries on earth, the same fault line every crisis finds. A country that cushioned its poorest in 2022 cut its energy poverty while prices tripled. A country that stopped cushioning them saw energy poverty return as soon as the support ended. The next war has already started sending its bill.

“As long as poverty, injustice and gross inequality persist in our world, none of us can truly rest.”

Nelson Mandela, Trafalgar Square, London, 3 February 2005
Method

Excess prices are actual Eurostat HICP prices for the Netherlands minus a path that grows 2% a year from the month each war began. Only food, home energy and motor fuel count. Wider "core" inflation would double the bill, but it had too many other causes to pin on a war. Each income fifth's spending pattern comes from the CBS budget survey; income from CBS household income tables. The energy channel uses the midpoint of the published and recomputed CBS energy series. Compensation not visible in prices (the €380 discount, the €1,300 energietoeslag) is subtracted per fifth.
