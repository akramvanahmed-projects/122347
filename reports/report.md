# Hidden casualties: what two war price shocks cost Dutch households

Every number in this report covers one period: from the invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 to August 2026, the latest month with price data, about four and a half years (March 2022 to August 2026, 54 months). Over that period the Russia–Ukraine war cost Dutch households €35.2 billion in excess spending on food, home energy and motor fuel, about €4,270 per household. Support, paid only in 2022 and 2023, returned €5.9 billion. After support, the middle three income fifths lost between 1.5% and 1.8% of their disposable income over the period, the richest fifth 1.0% and the poorest fifth 1.1%. The poorest fifth came out ahead while support lasted, then fell back under once it stopped and the prices did not. Translated into life expectancy, the net loss equals about 306 full lifetimes (range 123 to 613). Counted as casualties, the two wars together left about 2,100 estimated deaths from the cold over four winters, more than 900,000 people seriously wounded and about 3,050 lives lost in total once life-expectancy equivalents are added (range 750 to 6,400). The Iran war, inside the same period since March 2026, has so far cost €78 per household and the equivalent of 3 lifetimes. Its price shock has reached the petrol pump but not, as of August 2026, the gas meter.

These numbers come from a deliberately narrow definition of "war cost", and most of this report is about why it is narrow. The second half widens the lens to what money does not capture: the heating turned down, the holiday not taken, the record low in consumer confidence, and the attention the war absorbed. It then assembles everything into a casualty ledger of the dead, the wounded and the lost hours, and ends with who carried the load.

A companion story page presents the same results visually: `docs/index.html` in the repository.

## What is measured

A war shock raises some prices above where they would otherwise have been. For each category we take the price level in the month the war began and let it grow at 2% a year, the ECB target. The gap between actual prices and that path is the excess price. Prices are Eurostat's monthly HICP for the Netherlands (`prc_hicp_minr`, ECOICOP 2, 2015=100).

The period is the same for every number: March 2022 to the latest month with price data, now August 2026. Within it, the Ukraine shock has two phases. The acute phase runs to December 2023 and is measured month by month. After that, the price level the war left behind is still counted, but no later inflation is blamed on the war: each category's excess is capped at its October–December 2023 average and follows prices down if they fall. From the Iran shock in February 2026 the Ukraine excess is frozen, and Iran is measured on its own path anchored at February 2026 prices, so the two wars never claim the same price rise. Channels with no measurement for part of the period count zero there.

Only three categories count towards the headline: food, home energy and motor fuel. Those are the channels through which an energy-supply war reaches households directly. Excess inflation in everything else, mostly services and rents, is reported separately and kept out of the headline. In 2022 that "core" inflation had too many other causes to pin on the war: post-Covid reopening, the tightest Dutch labour market in decades, and indexation of rents. In 2026 Dutch services prices were already rising 4.2% a year in February, before the strikes (CBS, reported by NL Times, 2026), so a 2% path would blame Iran for a trend that predates it. Including core would add €2,532 per household to the Ukraine bill, more than doubling it. We do not believe that number.

Excess prices are weighted by what each income fifth buys, using the CBS budget survey of 2020 (StatLine 85420NED for shares, 85419NED for euro amounts). Owner-occupiers' imputed rent is removed from the spending base, because nobody pays it and HICP does not price it. Euro losses are compared with disposable income per fifth from CBS 83932NED, uprated by the all-items index after 2024. Compensation that does not appear in prices is subtracted per fifth: the €190 monthly discount in November and December 2022, and the €1,300 energietoeslag in 2022 and 2023. The 2023 price ceiling and the 2022 fuel excise cut are already inside the price index and are not counted twice (CBS, 2022).

## The Ukraine shock

The shock arrived in two waves. Home energy came first. On the published index it peaked 96% above the no-war path in October 2022, then fell to about 19% below it by late 2023, once the price ceiling and falling wholesale gas pulled it down, and stayed below it. Food came second and never retreated. It was still 14% above its path in December 2023 and is held at that level for the rest of the period. From January 2023 food was the larger cost every month (figure 4). Averaged across households, the monthly excess peaked at 4.8% of spending in December 2022 (figure 2).

Over the 54 months, food cost the average household €3,194 and home energy €1,423 (midpoint of the two energy series, see below). Motor fuel saved €351, mostly thanks to the excise cut and lower pump prices in 2023–2025. The energy figure is the most fragile: on the published index alone, home energy is a saving after 2023; on the CBS recomputation it is a cost throughout, because the level difference between the two methods carries forward.

The distribution depends on the denominator. As a share of spending the loss is nearly flat: 2.6% for the poorest fifth, 2.7% for the middle, 2.5% for the richest. The poorest fifth spends more of its budget on home energy (5.5% against 3.7%), but less on motor fuel (1.7% against 3.0%), and the two roughly cancel. As a share of income, the same losses look steeply regressive: 3.3% for the poorest fifth against 1.1% for the richest. The difference comes from one fact in the CBS data. In 2020 the poorest fifth spent €19,900 against a registered disposable income of €14,850, a ratio of 1.34. Part of that is real dissaving by students and pensioners drawing on assets. Part is probably income the survey misses [citation needed]. Either way, "the poor lost the most" is a statement about the denominator, not about who faced the higher prices.

Support changed the picture while it lasted. Every household received €380. Households near the social minimum could claim €1,300 in each of two years; most municipalities set the limit at 120% of the minimum and about a fifth at 130%. In the support years (March 2022 to December 2023), if 55% of the poorest fifth claimed both, the fifth as a whole ended €601 ahead, 1.9% of income. The break-even take-up for those years is 32% (figure 6). Municipalities estimate they reached about 90% of eligible households in 2022 and 86% in 2023 (SZW, 2024). At that reach, 55% take-up across the poorest fifth means roughly 60–64% of its households were eligible. The eligible share is not published by income fifth [citation needed], so this remains an important unverified number. A poorest-fifth household that missed the energietoeslag still lost about €830 in those two years, roughly 5% of a year's income. Support then stopped and the higher food prices did not: over the whole period the poorest fifth's net position turns into a loss of 1.1% of income, or €921 per household.

The CBS/TNO energy-poverty monitor points the same way. The share of households in energy poverty fell from 6.8% in 2021 to 4.1% in 2022 and 3.7% in 2023. Without support it would have been 5.9% and 8.0%. Once payments stopped, it went back to 6.0% in 2024 and stayed there in 2025.

## The Iran shock so far

Six months in (March to August 2026), Dutch motor fuel prices sit 15% above their no-war path. Food sits 1.3% below its path, and home energy only 2.6% above. Most households are on contracts that will reprice over the coming year, so the energy channel has barely started. The excess so far is €78 per household, €0.6 billion nationally, and 0.4% of monthly spending at the latest reading. That is a ripple next to Ukraine's wave (figure 2). It is also the one result in this project that will certainly change. The Action re-runs the model every month, and the end of the period moves with it.

The distribution runs the other way from Ukraine: as a share of spending the richest fifth loses 0.45% and the poorest 0.19%, because fuel is the channel and car ownership rises with income. The 2026 commuting-allowance increase is modelled with rough per-fifth amounts [citation needed], so the net Iran figures are less reliable than the gross ones.

## From income to life-years

CBS reports that the richest fifth lives about 8.5 years longer than the poorest (9 for men, 8 for women) and spends about 18 more years in good health [citation needed: income-only study year]. Read naively, that gradient implies that every percentage point of income lost costs life. Read carefully, it implies much less. Three adjustments do the work.

First, the gradient is cross-sectional and mostly not causal. Illness lowers income at least as much as low income causes illness, and education, occupation and health behaviour drive both. We credit 25% of the gradient to income, with 10% and 50% as bounds. These shares are judgement calls [citation needed]. Second, the gradient builds over a lifetime of income differences, so a 54-month period gets 54 months' worth of a 60-year exposure. Third, the gradient is expressed per log-point of standardised income, using the Q5/Q1 ratio of 4.8 in the 2024 CBS data. That assumes the same slope at every income level. US data show life expectancy rising steadily across the whole income distribution rather than only at the bottom (Chetty et al., 2016), which makes a single slope defensible but not proven for the Netherlands. The CBS gap is also measured on income and wealth together, while our ratio uses income alone, which steepens the slope somewhat.

The result for Ukraine over the period, after support, is 25,115 life-years and 53,184 healthy life-years across 18 million people. That works out to 0.52 days, about 12.6 hours, of life per resident. Divided by life expectancy, it equals 306 full lifetimes. Without support it would have been 391 (figure 5). For Iran so far: 3.

These are equivalences, not deaths. Nobody in this model dies. The figure says that a loss of life expectancy spread thinly over everyone adds up to the same total as about 306 complete lives. There is also serious evidence pointing the other way. In US state-level data, mortality falls during recessions (Ruhm, 2000), because people drive less, drink less and work less dangerously. A price shock is not a recession, but the sign of the short-run effect is not settled. The most direct mortality channel, cold homes, is estimated separately in the casualty ledger.


## Beyond the household budget

Money is the part of a war shock that statistics capture best. The rest has to be assembled from scattered sources, and for most of it the honest answer to "was this the war?" is "partly" or "we cannot tell". Each item below says which.

**The cold.** Dutch households burned about a quarter less gas in 2022 than in 2021, and total national use fell to 31 billion cubic metres, the lowest since 1972. Corrected for a mild winter, use was still 15% lower, and CBS names price as the main driver (CBS via NOS, 2023). Seven in ten households with a gas boiler set the thermostat to 19 °C instead of 21 (Milieu Centraal), and smart-thermostat data put the average living room at 18 °C that winter (tado; both via Wynia's Week, 2023). Whether the cold killed anyone is the open question of this project. In November 2022 about 1,400 more people died than expected, 10% above the norm, half of them in vulnerable groups such as nursing-home residents (CBS via ANP, December 2022). CBS attributes most of 2022's excess mortality to flu and covid-19 (CBS via ANP, January 2023), and no Dutch study separates out cold homes. The gas cut is clearly war-driven. The deaths cannot be assigned. *Verdict: can't tell.*

**The kitchen.** Food is the channel that stayed. It cost the average household €3,194 between March 2022 and August 2026 and was still 14% above its no-war path at the end of 2023, the level at which we hold it. Food banks served almost 200,000 people in 2022 and 180,579 in 2023; by 2025 that had fallen to just over 155,000 and was rising again (Voedselbanken Nederland, 2024; via NOS, 2026). Energy, fertiliser and grain push food prices up, but so did wages, retail margins and avian flu in the same period, so the food figure is an upper bound on the war's share. *Verdict: partly.*

**The holiday.** In 2023, 19.2% of people aged 15 and over took no holiday, against 18.5% in 2019. Of those who stayed home, 27.6% said they could not afford it. The gap by income is wide: 38% of the lowest-income quarter took no holiday, against 8% of the highest (CBS, 2024). Covid emptied travel in 2020 and the recovery hides any war effect in 2022. For Iran the link is direct: in April 2026 KLM scrapped 160 flights over the May holiday as kerosene prices rose (NL Times, 2026). *Verdict: can't tell for 2022, yes for 2026.*

**The nerves.** Consumer confidence fell to −59 in September and October 2022, the lowest since CBS began the series in 1986, against a twenty-year average of about −10 (CBS, 2022). In the same month Nibud reported that one in three households could barely make ends meet and that calls to its helpline ran 1.5 to 2 times above normal (Nibud via NL Times, 2022). The share of adults with many worries about their financial future rose from 22.5% in 2021 to 28.6% in 2022 and 28.7% in 2023, and was 26.1% in 2024, the latest year published (CBS, 2025); about 40% among the lowest earners (CBS via ANP, 2023). After the Iran strikes, confidence dropped from −24 in February 2026 to −46 in May (CBS, 2026). The timing matches both wars, but CBS does not attribute the drops to a cause. *Verdict: partly.*

**The attention.** In the first week of March 2022, 87% of Dutch adults followed the war, 90% were worried about it, and 44% feared a nuclear attack (I&O Research for NOS, n = 2,132). The eight o'clock news drew 2.3 million viewers on 7 March (TV Gids). No one has measured how many minutes the country spent following, discussing and worrying about the wars, so we assume it: 15 extra minutes a day per follower for the first three months, 5 a day for the rest of the first year, and 2 a day from March 2023 to the end of the period, across both wars. Applied to about 12.4 million adult followers, that is about 1.09 billion hours. Divided by a waking lifetime (82 years of 16 waking hours, about 479,000 hours), it equals about 2,280 waking lifetimes. The minutes are the weakest input in this report, and the story page lets the reader change them. Time spent understanding a war is not lost in the way a life is; much of it was civic attention, and some turned into donations and housing for refugees. Government time is not counted because neither cabinet nor parliament publishes hours by topic; the Speaker of the House described 2022 only as a year of "many debates" on Ukraine (Tweede Kamer, 2022). *Verdict: yes for the cause, assumed for the size.*

## The casualty ledger

The project's question was whether the wars caused human casualties in the Netherlands. Answering it in a military register means counting three things separately: the dead, the wounded, and, because a war that is followed on screens also consumes time, the hours.

**The dead: cold.** No Dutch death certificate names energy prices, so the count has to be estimated. Chirakijja, Jayachandran and Ong (2023) estimate from US winters that a 1% rise in the winter heating price raises all-cause mortality by 0.032% (standard error 0.014). In the Dutch heating season of October 2022 to March 2023, home energy cost on average 46% more than its no-war path, using the same midpoint of published and recomputed CBS energy prices as the cost model. About 82,000 deaths are expected in a six-month winter, a figure derived from CBS's weekly expected-death series (3,050 to 3,460 a week in December and January), and held at that level for later winters. The product for the first winter is about 1,200 deaths. The period contains three more winters, 2023–24, 2024–25 and 2025–26, in which heating still cost 10–11% more than the no-war path on the midpoint measure, adding about 300, 290 and 270. Over the four winters that is **about 2,100 deaths**, with a range of 300 to 3,800 from the 95% interval of the elasticity. Winters at or below the path count zero, and on the published index alone the three later winters would. For scale: CBS counted about 4,800 deaths above expected between late November 2022 and January 2023, attributing most of 2022's excess to flu and covid-19, and The Economist estimated 68,000 energy-price deaths across Europe that winter, which scales to a figure of similar magnitude for the Netherlands. Transferring a US elasticity to Dutch housing is the weakest step. Dutch homes are better insulated, but Dutch households cut gas use far more sharply.

**The dead: life expectancy.** The income channel described above adds 306 lifetime equivalents for Ukraine (123–613) and 3 for Iran, 309 in total.

**The wounded.** We count people whose quality of life the war damaged, separately from deaths, and price the damage in healthy life-years with disability weights from the Global Burden of Disease study (mild anxiety 0.030, moderate 0.133). Financial worry is not a GBD health state; we give it a third of the mild-anxiety weight and use the full weight as an upper bound. Serious hardship gets twice the mild weight. Each group is counted in every year of the period in which it was measurably larger than before the war; "People" is the largest such year and "Person-years" the sum.

People appear in several rows, so the counts are wounds, not persons. Together the groups lose about 54,700 healthy years, or **670 lifetime equivalents** (330–1,950). The serious wounded number about **904,306**; the light wounds about 17.4 million. The colder-home group carries no weight in the total: discomfort is counted, not converted. Years after 2024 count zero for money worries and after 2023 for holidays because no data is published yet; this understates the later part of the period.

**The ledger.** For the whole period, March 2022 to August 2026 and both wars, the dead from the cold plus the two life-expectancy channels give **about 3,050 lives** (range 750 to 6,400). Separately, and in a different unit, the attention the wars absorbed equals about **2,280 waking lifetimes**. These numbers should not be added to each other, and neither should be compared with the casualties in Ukraine or Iran, which are of a different order.

## Epilogue: who carried it

Every channel in this report bends the same way. Over the period the poorest fifth lost 3.3% of its income before support against 1.1% for the richest. It was nearly five times as likely to take no holiday (38% against 8%) and twice as likely to worry about money (about 40% against 20%). The households that fell through the support scheme were, by construction, in the bottom fifth. When support was generous, energy poverty fell during the worst price shock in fifty years; when it ended, energy poverty returned to 6% while the war's price level stayed.

"Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere," Martin Luther King Jr. wrote in 1963. The Dutch data are a small, measurable instance of the idea: a war in one place produced its injuries here along the existing lines of inequality. Nelson Mandela's version, from Trafalgar Square in 2005, is closer to a policy conclusion: "As long as poverty, injustice and gross inequality persist in our world, none of us can truly rest."

| Add excess core inflation | 7.04 | 3.04 |

Whole period, March 2022 to August 2026. Negative means the fifth came out ahead.

The energy series matters most, and more over the full period than over the acute phase. Before June 2023, Dutch energy prices were measured from new contracts only. CBS later recomputed the series with existing contracts. The recomputed CPI ran up to 8.1 index points below the published one in September 2022, and above it from January 2023. Anchored at February 2022, the published series undercounts what households on fixed contracts paid in 2023. The recomputed series counts the delayed pass-through of the autumn-2021 gas spike, which happened before the invasion, and that level difference carries into every later month. Each is biased, in opposite directions, so the central case takes the midpoint. The recomputation was published only as total CPI levels, so the correction is applied to the energy channel in proportion to each fifth's energy share.

The pre-trend counterfactual looks like the more data-driven option and is the worse one. Energy prices rose steeply between February 2020 and February 2022, so extrapolating that trend says the war *lowered* energy costs. The 2015 survey changes almost nothing. Including core inflation changes everything, which is exactly why it stays out of the headline.

## Limitations

The spending weights are from 2020, a lockdown year. Swapping in 2015 moves the results by about 0.1 percentage points, but neither year reflects how households cut gas use by about a quarter in 2022 (CBS via NOS, 2023). That makes our cash losses an upper bound, since we price the pre-crisis basket.

Quintile averages hide everything inside the quintile. A renter in a poorly insulated flat on a variable contract and a homeowner with solar panels can share a fifth. The budget survey is the only source that links spending to income, and CBS publishes it by fifth, not by household.

Income is registered disposable income, and pensions, wages and benefits were indexed to 2022–23 inflation with a lag. Indexation is a delayed form of compensation that this model leaves out. Over a four-and-a-half-year period this matters more than over the acute phase: by 2024 most collective agreements had caught up, so the net losses after 2023 are an upper bound for wage earners [citation needed].

The aftermath rule (holding each category at its late-2023 excess) is a judgement. It attributes the war's price level but none of the later inflation. A stricter rule would let the war's share decay as wages adjusted; a looser one would count all food inflation since 2022.

The CBS research series was transcribed from a CBS article, not downloaded from StatLine. It was checked against the inflation rates printed alongside it, but should be re-verified before anyone cites a single month.
